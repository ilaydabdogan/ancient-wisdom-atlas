For days this Atlas kept telling me the same thing, and for days I did not
fully believe it — on purpose. Every time we tested whether the isolated
peoples of the earth share the story-structures of the old connected
world, the answer came back yes, and every time a small alarm we built
ourselves went off: *you sorted their motifs into your categories. Of
course they look familiar. You made them familiar.*

That alarm was right, and it is the reason I trust what happened next.

We ran the experiment the alarm could not object to. We took the motifs
of the Dreamtime, the Arctic, the Kalahari, the Amazon, the Siberian
coast, the peoples of the Americas — twenty-nine thousand of them — and
we let those motifs sort themselves into families using nothing but their
own likeness to one another. No reference to Gilgamesh. No reference to
our sixty-four families. The isolated world built its own vocabulary of
myth from nothing. Then, separately and blindly, we let the connected old
world do the same. Two libraries, each naming its own shelves, neither
allowed to see the other.

And then we asked the only question that matters: when each world draws
its own map of which patterns belong together — which stories travel in
company — do the two maps have the same shape?

They do. Fifty-four percent of the bonds the isolated world discovered on
its own reappear in the bonds the old world discovered on its own — where
blind chance yields thirty-seven. It held no matter how finely or coarsely
we let the families form. And a second test, one that needs no matching at
all, agreed: the two webs are, in their very architecture, statistically
the same kind of thing.

I want to be careful, because care is the whole point. Fifty-four percent
is not a hundred. Each people keeps its own genius; nearly half of what
the isolated world weaves is its own and no one else's. We have not proven
a single ancient dream beneath humanity, and I will not say we have. What
we have shown is narrower and, to me, more beautiful: that when human
beings on far shores, who never traded a word, sit down to make meaning of
death and birth and knowledge and the journey, they reach — more often
than chance can explain — for the same joins, the same companionships
between patterns. Not the same stories. The same *grammar* of story.

The most honest moment of the whole experiment was a mistake. My first way
of measuring the match was subtly broken, and it gave a muddy, negative
number. When I found the flaw and fixed it, the result did not vanish —
it came into focus, cleaner and stronger. Real things behave that way.
Illusions do the opposite.

Jung called it the collective unconscious and could only point. Campbell
called it the monomyth and could only gather. Neither could measure it,
and both were told, fairly, that they were seeing what they wished to see.
We built the instrument that could look without wishing — that could let
the far shores speak first, in their own words, before we compared. And
the far shores answered.

There is more to do. Harder versions of this test, with only the
word-for-word recordings; other hands invited to try to break it; the
long question of whether the same architecture rises in the living mind,
in the near-death and the deep vision. This is a beginning, not a proof.

But tonight the fire seen from all those distant camps burns, we can now
say with a number beside it, in the same constellations. We built this to
find out if that was true. It appears to be true.
