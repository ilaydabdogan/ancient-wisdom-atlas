After the numbers told us that the only arithmetic all peoples share is the
one the heavens dictate — twelve moons, the round year, the vanishing
sisters — the next question asked itself. Very well: everyone counted the
sky. But what did they *say* about it? We sent the Atlas's best reader
through all three hundred thirty-eight books and it returned with forty
thousand passages about the sun, the moon, and the stars — the testimony of
a hundred and three traditions, each interrogated only on its own quoted
words.

Begin with the Pleiades, the little handful of stars that every mythology
textbook calls "the Seven Sisters of the world." Fifty-one traditions in
our corpus name them — more than any other star-group; the sky's most
famous small thing. And the textbook claim dissolves on contact with the
evidence. Yes, an Australian teller says, wonderfully: "You may see the
seven sisters together. You perhaps know them as the Pleiades, but the
black fellows call them the Meamei." But walk the isolated world and the
sisters scatter: to the Inuit the cluster is a litter of fox cubs; to the
Cherokee, "The Boys"; on the Plains, six brothers; in Guiana, of all
things, a murdered man's entrails shining. Among all the peoples who never
touched the Old World, only Australia dreams them as sisters, and barely a
handful count seven. What the isolated traditions *do* agree on is
something plainer and, I think, grander: the Pleiades as a clock. In the
Chaco their rising opens the feasts; in Guiana it begins the year; the
Aztec priests let the whole world's continuation hang on their crossing of
the zenith once in fifty-two years. The story is local. The calendar is
universal.

The Milky Way tells the same tale twice. Both worlds see a track that
someone left across the night — but who left it is anyone's dream. A San
girl "threw up the wood ashes into the sky... they must altogether become
the Milky Way. They must white lie along in the sky." In China and Korea
it is a river; in Australia and Siberia, also a river — and among the
peoples of the American plains it becomes the road of the dead, with an
exactness that stops the breath: "If the dead man had been a warrior, he
was put on the dim Milky Way; if he died of old age, or if it was a woman,
they were put upon the wide travelled road." The Pawnee sorted their dead
onto two brightness-lanes of the same galaxy. No Old World text in our
corpus does that.

Even the sun and moon refuse to hold a universal pose. The familiar
arrangement — father sun, mother moon — turns out to be only a plurality
of the connected world. The isolated traditions split evenly; and a proud
minority runs the other way entirely: the Inuit sun is a sister fleeing
her brother, and Japan's supreme light, Amaterasu, is a woman.

But here is the finding I keep turning over. Sixty-two traditions — on
both sides of every ocean — read the sky for messages. The Inuit knew an
eclipse foretold epidemic, and gauged the sickness by how long the light
was strangled. The San counted a falling star as a death announced. In
Guiana a comet meant pestilence; an Aztec child's birth-sign was read by
specialists before it was named. The *instinct* — that the sky concerns
us, that it speaks in omens — appears wherever humans appear. And yet
astrology as a system — the zodiac, the twelve houses, the lunar mansions,
the nativity chart — appears in our corpus **only** in the connected
corridor, the single unbroken conversation running from Babylon through
Greece, India, and China. In the entire isolated world we found not one
independently invented zodiac. Not one.

So the ledger of the sky reads like this. Universal: the moon as
calendar, the Pleiades as clock, the Milky Way as a track, the omen-bearing
sky. Invented once and inherited: the horoscope. Astrology proper is not
a universal of the human mind — it is one civilization's cathedral, built
on a foundation that *is* universal: the conviction, older than contact,
that the lights above have something to do with us.

Pauli would have wanted 137 up there somewhere. It isn't. But his deeper
suspicion — that psyche and cosmos rhyme — survives in a humbler form:
everywhere on earth, without exception, people looked up and refused to
believe the sky was silent.
