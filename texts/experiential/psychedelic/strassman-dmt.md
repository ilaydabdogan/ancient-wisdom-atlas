# Strassman DMT Experience Categories

In the course of clinical DMT studies at the University of New Mexico, Rick Strassman organized volunteer reports into a small number of recurring categories. The taxonomy below summarizes those categories in phenomenological language. The DMT experience is unusually compressed in time — the most intense portion typically lasts only a few minutes — but volunteers reported content of striking complexity, depth, and consistency across sessions.

## Threshold — Somatic Onset and Body Load

The first phenomenological territory is the body. Within seconds of administration, volunteers describe an intense rush — a wave moving up the body, pressure, vibration, sometimes a buzzing or humming. Anxiety often accompanies this onset; some volunteers feel they are dying, being pulled apart, or losing all volitional control. The somatic rush is rapid and overwhelming. It is followed within seconds by a perceptual transition out of ordinary reality.

## Personal — Emotional and Psychological Content

A subset of experiences remained, in content, recognizably about the volunteer's own life. Old emotional material — grief, fear, unresolved relationships — appeared and was felt acutely. In some cases volunteers had what they described as life-review-like processing of significant events. Memories were vivid; insight was felt as direct. The personal experiences were intense but contained, and the volunteer remained recognizable to themselves as themselves.

## Transpersonal — Beings, Entities, and Other Realms

A larger subset of experiences included encounter with beings — entities perceived as conscious, present, and other. The beings took many forms — humanoid, mechanical, insectoid, geometric, plant-like. Many volunteers described them as expecting them — as being already in progress with their own activities when the volunteer arrived. The beings communicated, sometimes in language, more often in direct transmissions of meaning. They were felt as more advanced, more knowing, or simply other. The volunteer's sense of being a particular human in a particular life sometimes thinned but did not always vanish.

## Transpersonal — Visual Geometry and Sacred Architecture

Many sessions opened on intricate geometric patterns of overwhelming complexity and beauty — fractal architectures, mandala-like forms, woven structures that themselves contained worlds. The geometry was felt as alive, as conscious, as an active environment rather than a passive image. Volunteers sometimes described temples, machines, halls, libraries, or architectures of light too complex to remember in detail.

## Transpersonal — Cosmic and Metaphysical Content

Some experiences contained content of a cosmological or metaphysical scale — the structure of reality, the nature of consciousness, the place of human life in a larger order. Volunteers reported direct apprehension of large-scale truths, sometimes with the sense that they had been shown something important and would have difficulty remembering it back in ordinary consciousness.

## Invisible Worlds — Consensus Alternate Realities

A distinctive feature of the DMT reports was the consistent description of an autonomous reality with its own rules, inhabitants, and consistency — a place that volunteers said was already there, would have continued without them, and could be entered repeatedly. Some volunteers described returning across multiple sessions to the same locations. Others described being recognized by the same beings on subsequent visits. The reports describe these realms not as inner imagery but as an apparently objective elsewhere, accessed during the few minutes that the substance is active.

## Invisible Worlds — Transmission and Instruction

In some sessions volunteers received what they described as transmissions — bodies of information delivered to them by the beings or by the structure of the place itself. The transmission was felt as compressed and deliberate. Volunteers sometimes returned with the conviction that they had been given a message, even when they could not recover the content.

## Return and Aftermath

The return is described as rapid. The structure of the experience collapses or recedes; ordinary perception resumes. Many volunteers reported lasting effects — changes in attitudes toward death, toward materialism, toward consciousness itself, and a continuing curiosity about whether the realms encountered have an existence apart from the volunteer's own mind.
