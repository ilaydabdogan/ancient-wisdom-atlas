# Interior Castle — The Seven Mansions

Teresa of Ávila describes the contemplative life as a journey inward through seven concentric mansions of the soul, with God dwelling at the center. The structure is phenomenological — each mansion is a state with its own characteristic experiences, struggles, and graces. Progress through the mansions is not linear; the practitioner may enter the further mansions briefly and return to the earlier, may oscillate, or may remain in one mansion for years. The summaries below describe the territory of each.

## The First Mansion — Self-Knowledge and Beginning

The soul enters the castle through prayer. The first territory is self-knowledge — a clear seeing of one's own state, including its disorder. The light from the inner mansions reaches here only dimly. The phenomenology is one of beginnings: a turning of attention toward the interior life, the recognition that something has been missed, an initial seriousness about prayer. Distractions are constant. The world's noise reaches into the mansion easily. The practitioner is still subject to vanity, anger, self-importance, and the ordinary movements of the heart, but these are now seen for what they are.

## The Second Mansion — Practice of Prayer Amid Struggle

The practitioner takes up prayer as a regular discipline. The struggle here is sustained — the will pulls toward distraction, toward the world's familiar consolations; the desire for God is real but not yet strong enough to override habit. The phenomenology is one of effort. Prayer is dry, intermittently consoled, often a matter of stubborn return. Spiritual reading and the company of others on the same path become important supports. The mansion is described as a place where many turn back, defeated by the difficulty.

## The Third Mansion — The Exemplary Life and Recollection

The practitioner has built habits of virtue and discipline. The life is ordered, charitable, prayerful, observant of duty. The phenomenology shifts: prayer has become a steady occupation rather than a struggle, and a quiet recollection of God during the day has become natural. The danger of this mansion is subtle complacency — the conviction that one has arrived, when one has only become a settled practitioner. The deeper graces lie ahead, but to receive them requires a willingness to be unsettled again.

## The Fourth Mansion — The Prayer of Quiet and Supernatural Beginnings

A new quality enters prayer — not produced by the practitioner's effort but received. Teresa distinguishes this from natural recollection: in the prayer of quiet, the will rests in God, even while thoughts may continue their usual movement. The phenomenology is one of inflowing peace, a sweetness that gathers around the heart, a contentment that does not depend on any object of thought. The will is held; the intellect and imagination may still wander but no longer disturb the deeper rest. Spiritual consolations begin — Teresa is careful to distinguish these from God himself, warning the practitioner not to mistake the gifts for the giver.

## The Fifth Mansion — The Prayer of Union and Spiritual Betrothal

The faculties are absorbed. The whole soul rests in God, who is now directly present rather than mediated by thought or feeling. The phenomenology is one of brief but unmistakable union — the practitioner emerges from prayer with a certainty that something has happened that was not produced by any of their faculties. Teresa uses the image of the silkworm dying in its cocoon and emerging as a butterfly: the natural self has been reconfigured, and the resulting orientation toward God is irreversible in a way the earlier states were not. The soul's longing for God is now sharp, almost painful in its intensity, even amid joy.

## The Sixth Mansion — Visions, Locutions, Raptures, and the Wound of Love

The mansion of intense graces and intense suffering. Visions occur — interior, imaginary, and rarely corporeal — bearing distinct phenomenological signatures that distinguish authentic visions from illusion. Locutions are heard — words received by the soul rather than spoken to the ears, marked by their authority, peace, and lasting effect. Raptures take the soul out of itself — a sudden, irresistible elevation in which the body becomes still, the senses suspended, and the soul caught up. The wound of love is constant — a longing for God so intense that it cannot be relieved by anything short of full union, which is not yet given. Public recognition, suspicion, slander, and physical illness often accompany this state externally; interiorly, the soul oscillates between sweetness and acute longing. The dark night Teresa describes here is one in which God is not absent but felt with an intensity that ordinary self cannot bear.

## The Seventh Mansion — Spiritual Marriage and Mystical Union

In the innermost mansion the soul is united with God in what Teresa calls spiritual marriage. The phenomenology shifts again: the wound of love quiets, replaced by a settled and continuous presence of God. Teresa describes a "mystical reach" in which God is present at the center of the soul as steadily as the practitioner is present to themselves. The faculties continue their ordinary work; the practitioner moves about in the world, attends to duties, suffers and serves; but the union holds. Action is no longer separate from prayer. The soul has become a place where divine and human meet without strain. The state is described as quiet rather than ecstatic — a peace that does not require unusual experiences, because the unusual has become the steady ground of the life.
