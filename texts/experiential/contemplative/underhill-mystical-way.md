# Underhill — The Five Stages of the Mystical Way

In her 1911 study Mysticism, Evelyn Underhill compared the testimony of mystics across the Christian centuries — and to a lesser extent across other contemplative traditions — and identified a recurring five-stage progression. The stages are not equally distributed: any given mystic may dwell longer in some, pass quickly through others, or oscillate. But the sequence appears with sufficient consistency across very different practitioners and centuries that Underhill treats it as the structural arc of the mystical life. The summaries below describe the territory of each stage.

## The Awakening of Self

The mystical life begins with an awakening. Some experience this as a sudden conversion — a moment in which the felt presence of the divine breaks into ordinary life and rearranges everything. Others come to it more slowly, through a gradual deepening of attention. The phenomenology is the same regardless of pace: a recognition that there is a real Other, that this Other is the deepest fact about reality, and that one's whole life has been turning around it without knowing. The awakening is accompanied by joy, certainty, and a sense of vocation. The world appears charged with significance it had not previously held. The mystic recognizes that the orientation that has just opened is permanent — not in the sense that the experience persists, but in the sense that one cannot return to a life unaware of what has been seen.

## The Purification of Self

The awakening reveals that the self is not yet fit to live in the new orientation. The purification of self is the long work of removing what stands in the way. The phenomenology is one of struggle — Underhill calls it the via purgativa. The mystic discovers, often painfully, the gap between what has been seen and what they actually are. Habits, attachments, vanities, fears, and unconscious self-importance are exposed and detached. Practices of asceticism, prayer, confession, and renunciation work directly on this material. The purification is felt as both relief and grief — relief at being unencumbered, grief at the loss of small comforts that had been mistaken for life. The stage may last years and is rarely complete; new layers of self surface as deeper layers are cleared.

## The Illumination of Self

Beyond purification opens a state of illumination. The mystic perceives the world as a manifestation of the divine; ordinary things — light on a wall, the texture of bread, the face of a passerby — disclose what they had always been. The phenomenology is one of saturated meaning. Joy is profound and steady. Prayer becomes contemplative; the imagination is unburdened; vision and locution may begin. The self is still present, and the divine is still encountered as an Other, but the encounter has become familiar. The mystic functions in the world with effectiveness, often takes up significant outward work, and is widely recognized for serenity, intelligence, and care. Underhill notes that some mystics never proceed beyond illumination, and that this is itself a complete contemplative life.

## The Dark Night of the Soul

For those who go further, illumination gives way to a state of acute deprivation. The felt presence of God is withdrawn. Prayer becomes dry; consolation disappears; the practices that were once living are suddenly empty. The phenomenology is one of abandonment — Underhill describes it as a stripping of all the supports the self had been using to relate to the divine. Despair, doubt, and a sense of being lost are common. Yet the dark night is not a regression. The deeper layers of self that illumination did not reach are now being addressed directly, and they cannot be addressed by anything the conscious mystic can do; they require the withdrawal of consolation. The mystic must continue the practice without the rewards that had previously sustained it. The stage is the most acute suffering of the mystical life. It may last years. It cannot be willed to end.

## The Unitive Life

The dark night ends in a stable union. The mystic now lives in continuous, settled communion with the divine. The phenomenology has changed: the alternation between presence and absence that marked the earlier stages is replaced by a steady ground that is neither felt nor unfelt in the old way, but is simply the medium of the life. The mystic does ordinary things — eats, sleeps, works, suffers, ages, dies — and the unitive life persists across all of it. Their action in the world becomes effective in a new way; Underhill emphasizes that the great mystics were not withdrawn but unusually productive, founders of orders, reformers, healers, writers. The fruit of the mystical way is not removal from the world but transparency to it. The unitive life is the end of the mystical sequence not because no more is possible but because the structure of the journey — out of ordinary self into the divine and back into the world transformed — is now complete.
