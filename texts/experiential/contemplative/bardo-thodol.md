# Bardo Thodol — The Three Bardos

The Bardo Thödol describes the conscious process of dying and the after-death period as a sequence of three intermediate states, called bardos. Each bardo is a distinct phenomenological territory with its own contents, opportunities, and dangers. The text is read aloud to the dying and the dead in order to orient the consciousness through what it will encounter. The three-stage structure below summarizes the territories the tradition describes.

## The Chikhai Bardo — The Moment of Death and the Clear Light

At the moment that the body dies, the dissolution of ordinary consciousness opens onto a luminous expanse. The first phenomenon is described as the Clear Light of pure awareness — vast, unobstructed, colorless, and self-radiant. It is not a light external to the experiencer but the unconditioned ground of awareness itself, ordinarily obscured by the activities of conditioned mind. If recognized, it offers immediate liberation. Recognition is rare because the light is unfamiliar — most consciousnesses, frightened by its vastness or unable to recognize it as their own nature, turn away.

A secondary clear light follows shortly after — gentler, more diffuse, still offering the possibility of recognition. The whole moment is described as brief, but the Tibetan tradition holds that for the prepared practitioner the duration is irrelevant, since the recognition is instantaneous if it is going to occur.

The phenomenology of this bardo includes: cessation of breath; sequential dissolution of the elements of the body, each accompanied by a felt and visualized correlate (earth dissolving into water, water into fire, fire into air, air into consciousness); the appearance of inner signs (a mirage-like shimmer, smoke, fireflies, a flame, a luminous expanse); and finally the unobstructed openness of awareness itself.

## The Chönyid Bardo — The Bardo of Reality

The consciousness that has not recognized the Clear Light enters the bardo of reality, in which the projections of mind appear as autonomous figures. Over a sequence of days the consciousness encounters peaceful deities and then wrathful deities — figures of overwhelming numinous quality, surrounded by intense colors, brilliant lights, and accompanying sounds.

The peaceful deities appear first. They emerge from the heart center as luminous, beautiful, compassionate figures, each accompanied by a particular wisdom-light corresponding to a specific aspect of awakened mind. The figures are vast, radiant, and complete — encountering them is felt as exposure to a presence so concentrated that ordinary self cannot tolerate it. Beside each deity a softer, dimmer light is also visible, pointing back toward conditioned existence. The consciousness is drawn toward the dimmer light because it is more recognizable; this is described as the danger of the encounter.

The wrathful deities appear next. They are described as terrifying — dark, fanged, surrounded by flames, ornamented with skulls and bones, holding implements of destruction. They are not actually different from the peaceful deities; they are the same wisdoms in a wrathful aspect, presenting themselves to consciousnesses that did not recognize the peaceful forms. The phenomenology is one of overwhelming fear, deafening sound, and the urge to flee. Recognition that these figures are projections of one's own awakened mind is the path to liberation; flight from them leads to further descent.

The whole bardo is characterized by colors of extraordinary saturation, lights that pierce, sounds like thousand-fold thunder, and a continuous opportunity for recognition that the consciousness is itself the source of what it is encountering.

## The Sidpa Bardo — The Bardo of Becoming

The consciousness that did not recognize the deities of the bardo of reality enters the bardo of becoming, in which it begins to seek a new birth. The phenomenology shifts. The consciousness now has a subtle body capable of moving instantly to any place it thinks of. It can pass through walls, through mountains, except for the womb that will be its next birth.

The consciousness encounters the karmic results of its life — visions of judgment, weighed deeds, terrifying landscapes, persecutors, and pursuers. It is buffeted by winds. It seeks shelter and finds the shelters illusory. It witnesses scenes that correspond to its own habits of mind. It looks back at the world it has left and finds it strangely closed off — relatives cannot hear it, food cannot be eaten.

Drawn by karmic affinity, the consciousness perceives the realms of possible rebirth — gods, demigods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell-beings — as scenes of extraordinary attraction or repulsion. It is drawn toward the scene that corresponds to its long-built habits. As the moment of conception approaches, the consciousness perceives its future parents and is pulled into the womb. With entry into the womb, this bardo ends and a new life begins, with no recollection of what has been traversed unless the practitioner has prepared the ground for memory to carry across.
