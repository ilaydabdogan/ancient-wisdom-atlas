# Patanjali Yoga Sutras — Eight Limbs and Samadhi Stages

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali describe the path of yoga as the cessation of the modifications of the mind, achieved through an eightfold structure of practice. The first five limbs prepare the ground — the ethical, postural, and energetic conditions that make stable concentration possible. The last three limbs unfold internal contemplative states of increasing depth, culminating in the various stages of samadhi. The summaries below describe the phenomenological content of each.

## Yamas — The Five Restraints

The path begins with five ethical disciplines that govern conduct toward others. Non-violence (ahimsa) — the cessation of harm in thought, word, and action. Truthfulness (satya) — the alignment of inner state, speech, and act. Non-stealing (asteya) — the absence of grasping for what has not been given. Continence (brahmacharya) — the conservation of vital energy. Non-possessiveness (aparigraha) — the absence of clinging to objects, status, or future. The yamas are not external rules but internal conditions; their stabilization quiets the mental disturbance that comes from violating them.

## Niyamas — The Five Observances

Five disciplines govern relation to oneself. Cleanliness (saucha) — purity of body, speech, and mind. Contentment (santosha) — a settled satisfaction independent of circumstance. Discipline (tapas) — sustained effort that purifies through friction. Self-study (svadhyaya) — examination of one's own mind and study of teachings that illuminate it. Surrender to the ultimate (ishvara pranidhana) — orientation of the whole life toward something greater than the personal self. The niyamas establish an interior ground from which deeper practice becomes possible.

## Asana — Posture

A stable, comfortable seat. The body is settled in a position that can be held without strain for the duration of practice. Breath becomes regular. The body fades from awareness as a source of distraction — neither denied nor indulged, simply quieted. Asana is the foundation for the inner work; without a stable body, the mind cannot stabilize.

## Pranayama — Breath Regulation

Conscious work with the breath. The phenomenology is felt — the breath becomes long, smooth, and subtle; gaps between exhale and inhale lengthen; the energy of the body settles and intensifies. Pranayama begins to draw the senses inward and prepares the ground for the inner limbs. The state induced by sustained pranayama is described as luminous and quiet, the mind no longer moved by its usual currents.

## Pratyahara — Sense Withdrawal

The senses turn inward. The eyes still see and the ears still hear, but attention is no longer pulled outward by sensory content. The phenomenology is one of growing interiority — the world that ordinarily occupies awareness recedes, and what remains is the field of mind itself. With pratyahara established, the practitioner has the conditions necessary for the three inner limbs that follow.

## Dharana — Concentration

Attention is fixed on a single object — an image, a sound, a region of the body, the breath, a sacred name. The mind returns to the object whenever it wanders. Initially this is effortful; with practice the return becomes faster and the wandering shorter. The phenomenology is one of training — the developing capacity to hold attention where the practitioner directs it, undisturbed by the passing currents of thought.

## Dhyana — Meditation

Concentration becomes continuous. The object of attention is held without interruption; the return is no longer needed because the attention no longer leaves. The practitioner and the object are still distinct, but the gap between them has narrowed. The phenomenology is one of unbroken contemplation — a steady stream of attention rather than the pulsed return of dharana.

## Samadhi — Absorption

Distinction between subject and object dissolves. The practitioner is absorbed into the object of meditation; the structure of "one who meditates" and "what is meditated upon" gives way. Samadhi is described as having sub-stages of increasing depth.

### Savitarka Samadhi — Absorption with Gross Reasoning

The simplest samadhi. The mind is absorbed into the object, but verbal and conceptual reasoning about the object continues. The object is grasped together with its name, its concept, and its associations.

### Nirvitarka Samadhi — Absorption Without Gross Reasoning

The verbal-conceptual layer falls away. The object is grasped directly, without the mediation of name and concept. The phenomenology is one of immediate, naked perception of what is — a knowing without the chatter of categories.

### Savichara Samadhi — Absorption with Subtle Reasoning

Absorption into subtle objects, with subtle reasoning continuing. The object of contemplation has become finer — a subtle element, a quality of mind itself.

### Nirvichara Samadhi — Absorption Without Subtle Reasoning

The subtle reasoning falls away. What is grasped is the subtle object itself, without any layer of conceptual processing. Patanjali describes this state as bringing a particular kind of clear, unmistaken knowledge.

### Sananda Samadhi — Absorption with Bliss

A samadhi suffused with bliss. The contemplative state itself becomes the object; the bliss-quality of awareness is grasped directly.

### Sasmita Samadhi — Absorption with the Sense of Pure Self

Awareness rests on the bare sense of "I am" — the most fundamental layer of self-experience, prior to any content of selfhood. The phenomenology is one of resting in pure subjectivity.

### Asamprajnata Samadhi — Seedless Samadhi

The deepest samadhi. The objects of awareness are gone; even the sense of pure self is gone. What remains is unconditioned awareness, with no content. Patanjali describes this as the state in which the long-running modifications of mind cease, and the practitioner stands in their true nature — pure, witnessing consciousness, no longer mistaken for the contents that had moved through it.
